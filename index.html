Four generations of the Li family

One Hunan landlord family, from 1910 to today, told by its fourth generation.

1910 to 2026

About this history

For more than a hundred years, one family from the countryside of Taoyuan County in Hunan has lived through every turn of modern China: the end of the empire, war, revolution, reform, and the long boom. None of them chose the times. Each generation made its choices inside them.

I am the fourth generation. This site records what my family has told me, before it exists only in memory. It does not judge the events it describes. It follows what they did to four lives.

Names are pseudonyms. Photographs are period images from public archives, used because family photographs are not available; none of them show the Li family.

Generation 1of 4

The great-grandfather, born 1910

Li Hanqing 李翰卿

My great-grandfather was born in 1910, a year before the revolution that ended two thousand years of imperial rule. The news of a republic changed little in the villages of Taoyuan. The countryside still ran on small family farms, and order was kept by local gentry and by the clans.

His parents had built up some property through hard work and careful spending. That head start, and a country that was slowly opening, let him do what his parents could not: he went on to higher education. He came back with new ideas about running an estate and the means to act on them.

The family’s holdings grew, mostly in farmland, and the Li household became one of the known landlord families of the area. Between the late 1930s and the late 1960s he had seven children. One son died young.

Generation 2of 4

The eldest son, born 1939

Li Zhiyuan 李致远

My grandfather was the eldest son. He was born in 1939, when the family was at its height, and he grew up in a comfortable house with enough of everything.

In 1949 the civil war ended and the People’s Republic was founded. The years of class struggle that followed turned the family’s position upside down. As a landlord, my great-grandfather was sent to prison. The house and the land were confiscated. A family that had stood near the top of local society now stood near the bottom, and it went through one political campaign after another. For years the household had no settled place.

My grandfather and his elder sister eventually left home on their own, he to study in Wuhan and she in Changsha. The four younger siblings stayed and worked the land.

In the early 1980s, as the country turned toward reform and opening, the government’s restitution policy reached the family. Part of the property was returned, and they moved from the village into the local town. My grandfather and his sister, both university educated, stayed in Wuhan and Changsha, found work, married, and raised children there. The second son farmed in the home village. The second daughter married young and moved far away. The third son and third daughter found work in the town.

Only the two eldest children had been born early enough to go to school. The four younger ones never had the chance.

Generation 3of 4

My parents’ generation, born 1973

My parents’ generation was born in 1973. By the time they were old enough for school, the country had reopened its universities and begun to open to the world, so they received a complete education from primary school through university.

When they started work, the economy was growing fast. Over the years they rose to become the experienced core of their workplaces and joined the new urban middle class. Theirs was the generation that collected what the times had to offer.

Generation 4of 4

My generation, born in the 2000s

My generation was born in the early 2000s, into a China growing at full speed. We have never been short of things. We have been short of room: every step, from school places to jobs, is a competition, and people now call it neijuan, involution.

China is also far more connected to the rest of the world than it was for my parents. With their support, many of us have gone abroad to study, and some of us will change not only country but identity. I went from Wuhan to Montreal, then to Stanstead, and now to Waterloo.

My great-grandfather’s education made the family. Two generations later, education was the one thing my grandfather kept when everything else was taken. It is the thread I am still following.

    How this was made

    Sources

    The narrative comes from the family’s own account, written down by me in 2026. Dates given as years (1910, 1939, 1973) come from that account. Where the account gives no date, for example the year my great-grandfather was imprisoned, the site does not supply one.

    What is substituted

    There are no family photographs, household registers, or letters in this version. Each photograph is a public-domain or openly licensed image from the same region or era, captioned with its real place and date. Family names are pseudonyms.

    What comes next

    The next step is recorded interviews with each living generation, asking everyone the same questions: why they moved, what they feared, what they valued. Transcripts would let the word section count what people actually say, instead of relying on my selection.

    A note on perspective

    This history is told by the youngest generation. It is shaped by what I was told and by what the older generations chose to pass on. That filtering is part of the subject, not a flaw to hide.
