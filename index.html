One Hunan landlord family, from 1910 to today, told by its fourth generation.

About this history

For more than a hundred years, one family from the countryside of Taoyuan County in Hunan has lived through every turn of modern China: the end of the empire, war, revolution, reform, and the long boom. None of them chose the times. Each generation made its choices inside them.

I am the fourth generation of my mother’s family. This site records what my family has told me, before it exists only in memory, and asks one question: how did land reform, industrialization, and urbanization shape the paths of a single family? It does not judge the events it describes. It follows what they did to four lives.

People are identified by their family role and the surname Li, not by name, and the home village, the college, and the steel plant are not named. Photographs are period images from public archives, used because family photographs are not available; none of them show the Li family.

Generation 1of 4

The great-grandfather, born 1910

Great-grandfather Li 外曾祖父

My great-grandfather was born in 1910, a year before the revolution that ended two thousand years of imperial rule. The news of a republic changed little in the villages of Taoyuan. The countryside still ran on small family farms, and order was kept by local gentry and by the clans.

His parents had built up some property through hard work and careful spending. That head start, and a country that was slowly opening, let him do what his parents could not: long before 1949, he went on to higher education. He came back with new ideas about running an estate and the means to act on them.

The family’s holdings grew, mostly in farmland, and the Li household became one of the known landlord families of the area. Between the late 1930s and the late 1960s he had seven children. One son died young.

The war against Japan reached the family in 1945. They fled west to Cili County for about six months, leaving the property behind, and then came home to Taoyuan.

Land reform reached Taoyuan in 1951. The family was classified as landlords, the property was confiscated, and my great-grandfather was sent to prison. He was released in 1955 and returned to Taoyuan for three years of labour reform. In 1981 his status was restored and he retired.

Generation 2of 4

The eldest son, born 1939

Grandfather Li 外公

My maternal grandfather was the eldest son. He was born in 1939 in a village in Taoyuan, in the middle of the war. His first classroom was a private school in the home village, which he started at five, in 1944.

In 1945 the family fled to Cili County for about half a year. When they came home he went back to the same private school, and later on to primary school. In 1951 land reform took everything: the property was confiscated, the household was left bankrupt, and his father was in prison. That same year my grandfather finished primary school and left for middle school in Changde. Times were hard, and he had only four years of it.

In 1955, at sixteen, he was admitted to an engineering college in Wuhan to study construction engineering, one of the first two of his generation to reach university. The same year, his father was released from prison and returned to Taoyuan for labour reform.

He graduated in 1959 and joined a state steel plant in Ezhou, just east of Wuhan, as a technician. Over forty-one years he worked in building design, construction, and project management, and rose to senior engineer before he retired in 2000.

In 1963, because of his father’s history, he cut off contact with his family in Taoyuan. For eighteen years there were no visits and no letters. Only in 1981, when his father’s status was restored, did they reconnect.

His sister, the eldest daughter, also left home to study, in Changsha, and settled there. The four younger siblings stayed and worked the land. In the early 1980s the restitution policy returned part of the property and the family moved into the local town. The second son farmed in the home village, the second daughter married young and moved far away, and the third son and third daughter found work in the town.

Only the two eldest children had been born early enough to go to school. The four younger ones never had the chance.

Eighteen years without contact, 1963 to 1981.

Generation 3of 4

My parents’ generation, born 1973

My parents’ generation was born in Ezhou in 1973, in the steel town where my grandfather worked. By the time they were old enough for school, the country had reopened its universities and begun to open to the world, so they received a complete education from primary school through university.

In 1991 they moved to Wuhan, the provincial capital next door, and put down roots there.

When they started work, the economy was growing fast. Over the years they rose to become the experienced core of their workplaces and joined the new urban middle class. Theirs was the generation that collected what the times had to offer.

Generation 4of 4

My generation, born in the 2000s

My generation was born in the 2000s, into a China growing at full speed. We have never been short of things. We have been short of room: every step, from school places to jobs, is a competition, and people now call it neijuan, involution.

China is also far more connected to the rest of the world than it was for my parents. With their support, many of us have gone abroad to study, and some of us will change not only country but identity. I left Wuhan for Montreal in 2022, moved to Stanstead in 2023, and came to Waterloo in 2026.

My great-grandfather’s education made the family. Two generations later, education was the one thing my grandfather kept when everything else was taken. It is the thread I am still following.

Part of a larger migration

One family’s moves sat inside a much larger movement of people between countryside and city.

  1. 1955

    China. From October 1955 about 100,000 builders from across the country arrived in Wuhan to build its steel works, backed by 18 provinces, 45 cities and more than 1,000 factories.

    The family. That same year my grandfather arrived in Wuhan to study construction engineering. Four years later he joined the steel industry himself, at a plant in Ezhou.

  2. 1958

    China. New household registration rules fixed every person as rural or urban, while the Great Leap Forward drew millions of villagers into city factories.

    The family. With a college place, my grandfather was on the urban side of that line. His four younger siblings, without schooling, stayed on the land.

  3. 1961

    China. From 1961 to mid-1963 the state cut 18.87 million workers and reduced the urban population by 26 million, sending mostly recent rural recruits back to the countryside.

    The family. As a trained engineer, my grandfather kept his place in the city. Education decided not only who left the village, but who could stay away.

Sources: Qingshan District Government, Wuhan, on the building of Wuhan Iron and Steel; Institute of Party History and Literature of the CPC Central Committee, on the 1961-1963 reduction of urban population.

      Land reform

      Land reform did not only take the family’s land. It gave every member a class label, and that label decided who could go to school. The two children born before 1949 had already started; the four born later never did. Birth order became destiny.

      The label also reached into private life. In 1963, because of his father’s history, the eldest son cut off contact with home, and the silence lasted eighteen years, until his father’s status was restored in 1981. The same classification that took the property shaped who the family could stay close to.

      Industrialization

      In the 1950s the state built heavy industry across central China, with Wuhan at its centre. The eldest son’s life followed that plan closely: a construction engineering course in Wuhan from 1955, then forty-one years at a state steel plant in Ezhou, from technician to senior engineer. A college place was one of the few routes from a confiscated village household into that industrial workforce.

      After 1978, market reform turned that foothold into something larger. His children, born in Ezhou in 1973, grew up as the economy took off and became part of the new urban middle class.

      Urbanization

      From 1958, household registration fixed people as rural or urban. For a generation the family stayed split along that line: the two educated children in cities, four on the land. On the chart, the siblings’ urban share holds at two in six until the reforms of the early 1980s move two more into town.

      Each later move climbed one step further: from a steel town to the provincial capital in 1991, then from Wuhan to another country in the 2020s. By the time China became a majority-urban country in 2011, the eldest son’s line had been urban for sixty years.

      National change set the options. Inside the family, the timing of a birth and access to education decided who crossed the line from countryside to city.

      How the counting works

      1. GroupTexts are sorted by generation, 2 to 4.
      2. CleanPunctuation and blank lines are dropped; names of people and places are withheld.
      3. Segmentjieba splits Chinese text into words, with every codebook term added to its dictionary so terms stay whole.
      4. MatchEach word is checked against the four-dimension codebook.
      5. CountMatches are tallied by generation and dimension.
      6. CompareCounts are set side by side across generations, as in the chart above.

        How this was made

        Sources

        The narrative comes from two family accounts, written down by me in 2026. Years such as 1939, 1951, 1955, 1959, 1963, and 1981 come from those accounts. Where they give no date, for example the year the eldest daughter left for Changsha, the site marks the year as approximate.

        What is substituted

        There are no family photographs, household registers, or letters in this version. Each photograph is a public-domain or openly licensed image from the same region or era, captioned with its real place and date. People are identified by family role and surname rather than by name; the home village, the college, and the steel plant are not named.

        Data

        Everything on the map, the network, and the charts, apart from the keyword counts, comes from one structured file of people, places, residences, moves, events, and relationships. Years marked approx. are estimates placed for mapping. Download: people, places (GeoJSON), moves (GeoJSON), residences, events, relationships, or everything (JSON).

        Keyword analysis

        Each generation’s texts are segmented with jieba, a Chinese NLP tokenizer, after every keyword in the codebook has been added to its dictionary so that terms stay whole. The script then counts exact token matches and the most frequent nouns and verbs. The interview transcripts are kept private; the site shows only counts and corpus sizes.

        A note on perspective

        This history is told by the youngest generation. It is shaped by what I was told and by what the older generations chose to pass on. That filtering is part of the subject, not a flaw to hide.
